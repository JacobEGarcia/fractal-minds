WHEN A REASONING MODEL THINKS HARDER, ITS THOUGHTS BECOME A DYNAMICAL SYSTEM WITH FRACTAL BOUNDARIES. THIS PAGE LETS YOU WATCH THAT HAPPEN.

01 / THE CLAIM

Reasoning models think longer on harder problems. Why? Gilpin's group shows the thinking itself is a dynamical system: each step of reasoning revisits and revises an attempted answer. On easy problems the iteration falls straight into the right answer. On hard problems it gets trapped near saddle points - nearly-correct solutions that almost work - and wanders chaotically between them before escaping. The map of "which answer you land on, given where you started" has fractal boundaries, and the fractality grows with task difficulty. Below: two live demonstrations you can poke.

02 / WATCH A SOLVER THINK (SUDOKU)

A min-conflicts solver is a tiny reasoning model: it holds a full candidate answer, finds where it contradicts itself, and revises one cell at a time, starting hot and exploratory, then cooling toward an answer - like a reasoning trace settling. The plot is its thought trajectory - total contradictions over time. Easy puzzle: straight to the answer. Hard puzzle: long chaotic wandering, hovering near almost-right grids (conflicts = 1 or 2) that are exactly the paper's saddle points. Hit PERTURB ONE CELL mid-thought: a one-cell change sends the whole trajectory somewhere else. That sensitivity is the chaos.

03 / THE FRACTAL MAP OF ANSWERS (BASIN EXPLORER)

Here is the paper's fractal made literal. Newton's method is the simplest iterative reasoner: it starts from a guess and repeatedly improves it until it lands on a root of z^3 - 1. There are three correct answers (three roots). Color every starting guess by which answer it reaches: orange, teal, cream. The boundary between colors is not a line - it is a fractal. Click to zoom into any boundary: it keeps branching, forever. Near the boundary, an infinitesimally different first guess gives a completely different final answer. That is what a hard problem does to a reasoning trace.
